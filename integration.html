L4 · From slope to surface

Zonal and modal integration, noise growth, and what slopes cannot see.

Learning objective
Deflectometry measures slope; the shape is its integral. After this module you can integrate a slope profile two ways (zonal and modal), explain why noise grows when slopes are integrated, and say what deflectometry cannot see: piston, and steps between segments.

Key ideas
  • Zonal integration sums neighbouring slopes (in 2-D, a least-squares grid: Southwell 1980, ref. 14 of Choi et al.). It keeps every detail but accumulates noise like a random walk.
  • Modal integration fits a few smooth functions (polynomials, Zernikes). It filters noise but cannot represent features finer than its highest order.
  • A slope bias integrates into a tilt of the reconstructed surface — calibration errors look like real tilt.
  • Piston is invisible: a constant height has zero slope. A step between two segments appears only as a spike at the gap, where there is no data. This is why SMOTS measures segment tip/tilt, and why the paper notes that slope sensors such as the Shack–Hartmann are insensitive to piston.
Check yourself
1. Double the samples across D with the same slope noise. Does the zonal error grow or shrink?

For a fixed aperture the random-walk error is σs·Δx·√n = σs·D/√n, so finer sampling reduces it — each slope sample covers a shorter step. (In practice σs per pixel also rises as pixels get smaller.)

2. Add a 10 µrad bias over D = 50 mm. What height error appears, and of what shape?

A linear tilt of 10 µrad × 50 mm = 500 nm end to end. It is indistinguishable from a real tilt without an absolute reference — the "absolute reference" calibration Choi et al. mention.

3. Choose "two segments with a piston step". Why does neither method recover the step?

Inside each segment the slope is the same as without the step; the step lives only in the gap, where nothing is measured. Deflectometry can align segment tilts, but co-phasing (piston) needs another method.

4. Choose "local bump" and lower the modal order. What do you see, and why?

The modal fit cannot follow the bump: low-order polynomials are too smooth. The zonal result follows it but carries more noise. Choosing the method is choosing between detail and noise.
